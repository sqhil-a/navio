Career resources

Three practical guides you can use today.

Original, no-cost career exploration tools for Ontario secondary students, families, and educators. No account or download is required.

Start the research guide

Guide 1 · Career research

Investigate the work, not only the title.

A career title can hide very different daily experiences. Start by writing what you think the role involves, then test those assumptions using several reliable sources. Look for descriptions from professional associations, education or training providers, labour-market sources, and people who have performed the work.

  1. Define the tasks: List recurring responsibilities, the kinds of problems solved, and who receives or evaluates the work.
  2. Study the environment: Note whether work is independent or collaborative, remote or in person, predictable or variable, desk-based or physical.
  3. Compare routes: Identify required and common education, training, licensing, portfolios, experience, or apprenticeships. Distinguish a legal requirement from an employer preference.
  4. Look for tradeoffs: Consider schedule, location, cost of preparation, income variability, emotional demands, physical demands, and opportunities to advance or change direction.
  5. Check more than one source: Record where each claim came from and when it was updated. Treat promotional pages as one perspective, not the complete picture.
  6. Choose a test: Find a small project, course, conversation, observation, club, or volunteer task that lets you experience one part of the work.

Research note

Complete this sentence: “I was attracted to this field because ____. After researching the daily work, I now want to learn more about ____.”

Guide 2 · Professional conversations

Ask questions that reveal experience.

An informational conversation is for learning, not asking a stranger for a job. Begin by explaining why you are curious, how much time you are requesting, and that you want to understand the field. Use public or appropriately supervised communication channels and respect a person’s decision not to participate.

Questions about the work

  • What does a normal week look like, and which parts are least visible to students?
  • Which task takes more time or judgment than people expect?
  • What kinds of problems do beginners usually encounter?
  • How do people in this field receive feedback and know their work is effective?
  • Which work environments or schedules are common?

Questions about preparation

  • Which skills should a student begin practising before post-secondary education or training?
  • Which credentials are required, and which are simply common?
  • What did your own route look like, and what would you approach differently?
  • What is one low-risk way to test whether I enjoy a real part of this work?

After the conversation, send a short thank-you, record what changed in your understanding, and verify factual claims—such as licensing rules or admission requirements—through an authoritative source.

Guide 3 · Decision reflection

Compare evidence before choosing a next step.

Career exploration is not a one-time choice. Use this reflection after research, a workshop, a course, or a conversation. It helps separate what you learned from what you still assume.

Interest

Which tasks or topics held your attention? Was your interest in the actual work, the social impact, the status of the title, or something else?

Fit

Which work environments, schedules, collaboration styles, and learning demands seem compatible with how you currently work?

Evidence

Which sources support your understanding? Where did sources disagree, and which questions remain unanswered?

Tradeoffs

What costs, constraints, uncertainties, or less appealing tasks would you need to accept or investigate?

Write a next-step statement

Use this format: “Over the next __ weeks, I will __ because I need to learn whether __. I will review what I learned on __.” A useful next step is specific, time-limited, and focused on learning rather than proving that your first idea was correct.

Educational use: These resources provide general guidance. They do not guarantee admission, employment, salary, volunteer-hour eligibility, or another outcome. Verify program, licensing, and labour-market requirements with current authoritative sources.
