Career conversation guide · 20 minutes

Ask a professional questions your research cannot answer.

An informational conversation is a short learning exchange. It is not a request for a job, a private mentorship, or a promise of ongoing access.

Open worksheet 4All resources

Decide what the conversation should teach you

Complete basic research first. A professional should not have to explain information already available on a public page. Review your Career Evidence Brief and circle one gap: a task you cannot picture, a tradeoff that sources describe differently, a beginner skill you want to test, or a transition between education and work that remains unclear.

Write one sentence that defines success. For example: “After this conversation, I want to understand how much of a normal week involves writing, meetings, and independent problem-solving.” A focused purpose makes it easier to choose the right person and respect their time.

Choose an appropriate contact route

Start with someone already connected through a school, family, community organization, supervised activity, public professional event, or official workplace channel. Students should involve a parent, guardian, educator, guidance counsellor, or trusted adult where appropriate. Use a public or appropriately supervised channel; do not move to an unexpected private account or arrange an unsupervised meeting.

Send a limited, honest request

Message template: Hello, my name is ______ and I am an Ontario secondary student researching ______. I have read ______, but I still want to understand ______. Would you be willing to answer five questions in a 15-minute conversation through ______? I am looking for information, not a job or ongoing mentoring. It is completely fine if you are unavailable. Thank you for considering it.

Personalize the request. Name the connection or public source that led you to the person. Ask once and allow the person to decline. Do not send repeated messages, request personal contact details, or imply that a school or Navio has endorsed the outreach.

Prepare five questions with different jobs

  1. Reality: What does a normal week include, and which responsibility is least visible to students?
  2. Entry: Which beginner skill or experience helped you become useful in the role?
  3. Tradeoff: What part of the work requires the most patience, energy, or adjustment?
  4. Variation: How can the same job title differ across workplaces or stages of a career?
  5. Test: What is one safe, small activity that could help a student test a real part of this work?

Replace generic questions with ones tied to your evidence. Instead of asking “What education do I need?”, say, “I found two routes with different amounts of practical learning. In your experience, which early tasks best showed employers that a beginner could do the work?”

During the conversation

Begin by confirming the available time and ask permission before taking notes. Keep personal questions out of the conversation. Do not ask for confidential workplace information, information about clients or patients, or promises of admission or employment. If a response describes a licence, credential, wage, or formal requirement, record it as a lead to verify—not as the final authority.

Turn the conversation into evidence

Within a day, send a short thank-you that names one useful idea. Add three notes to your portfolio: what you learned, what surprised you, and what needs confirmation. Verify formal facts on the responsible regulator, school, employer, or government site. Then decide whether the conversation changed your criteria, pathway comparison, or experience-test plan.

Know when to stop

End the exchange if boundaries change, the person asks for unnecessary personal information, or the conversation no longer feels appropriate. Do not accept unplanned work, travel, private meetings, or account access. A young person can share a concern with a trusted adult and use Navio's public youth-safety contact route when the activity involves Navio material.
