Career experience guide · 20 minutes

Test one assumption before making a large commitment.

A good experience test is small enough to complete, realistic enough to teach you something, and safe for your age and setting.

Open the test plannerAll resources

Test a question, not an entire career

One afternoon cannot prove that a career is right or wrong. It can test a narrower assumption: Do I enjoy explaining technical information? Can I stay engaged while improving a design through several revisions? How do I respond to a task that requires precise measurement? The result should help you choose a better next question.

Begin with a claim from your Career Criteria Card or Evidence Brief. Choose one that matters to your decision and can be explored without pretending to perform regulated, hazardous, confidential, or age-restricted work.

Write the five parts of the test

  1. Question: Name the assumption you want to examine.
  2. Task: Choose one observable activity connected to the skill or work pattern.
  3. Evidence: Decide what you will save or record: a draft, checklist, time log, feedback, error count, or reflection.
  4. Boundary: Set the time, cost, supervision, privacy, safety, and communication limits.
  5. Decision date: Choose when you will review the evidence and select a next step.
Test statement: To learn whether ______, I will complete ______ by ______. I will record ______. I will keep the activity safe by ______. On ______, I will decide whether to continue, adjust, or compare another option.

Examples that stay within a student scope

  • Technology: Build a simple, accessible information page and ask two people whether they can complete a specific task. Test whether you enjoy debugging and revision—not whether you are already employable.
  • Communications: Turn one public report into a one-page explainer for a defined audience. Record editing decisions and feedback.
  • Skilled trades: Attend an official school, college, union, or community demonstration with appropriate supervision. Observe tools and procedures without performing restricted work.
  • Health or community service: Complete a public introductory learning activity or supervised, age-appropriate service task that does not involve patient access, confidential records, or clinical work.
  • Public service: Read a public meeting agenda, summarize one issue, and compare your questions with the published minutes or recording.

The activity does not need to copy the full occupation. It should expose you to a recurring skill, condition, or tradeoff in a responsible way.

Collect evidence during the activity

Record what happened rather than relying on your mood at the end. Note when your attention increased or dropped, which task took longer than expected, what feedback changed the result, and which constraint mattered. Separate discomfort caused by being a beginner from dislike of the underlying work. A first attempt can be difficult and still reveal genuine interest.

Use a three-part reflection

  1. Continue: Which part do you want to practise or investigate again?
  2. Adjust: Which condition, scope, or support would create a fairer test?
  3. Compare: Which alternative field or pathway might meet the same criteria differently?

Choose one result and attach a dated action. “Research more” is not specific enough. A useful action names the source, person, task, or program you will examine and the question it should answer.

Safety and privacy boundaries

Follow the rules of the school, organization, workplace, parent or guardian, and supervising adult. Do not access equipment, sites, accounts, client information, patient information, or private records without authorization. Do not represent yourself as qualified. Stop an activity if its scope changes or the environment becomes unsafe.
