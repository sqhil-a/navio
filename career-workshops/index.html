Available now · no registration

Turn a broad career interest into questions you can investigate.

A free self-guided toolkit for Ontario secondary students in Grades 9–12. Work through six practical modules at your own pace; no account or personal information is required.

Start module oneUse companion guides
You can begin now: Read each module, write your answers in a notebook or private document, and complete the action plan at the end. Navio does not collect your responses.

Toolkit purpose

A practical introduction to career exploration.

This toolkit is for students who have several interests, one early idea, or no clear direction yet. It does not ask you to select a permanent answer. Instead, it teaches a sequence you can repeat whenever you need to investigate an option.

You will separate a career title from the daily work behind it, compare education or training routes, identify skills you can test now, evaluate tradeoffs, and choose one low-risk next action. The goal is a better-informed question—not a guaranteed outcome.

Module 1 · Choose a question

Begin with curiosity, not a permanent choice.

Pick one career or field you want to understand better. Write what first attracted you to it, then list three things you currently assume about the work. An assumption might concern the daily tasks, education, income, schedule, impact, work setting, or the kinds of people who succeed.

Your prompt

  1. What attracts me to this field?
  2. Which parts of the actual work do I understand?
  3. Which beliefs are based on evidence, and which came from impressions?
  4. What single question would most change my view?

Module 2 · Describe the work

Look beyond the job title.

Find reliable descriptions of recurring responsibilities, problems solved, people served, work environments, schedules, and ways performance is evaluated. Use at least three sources and note when each was updated. Prefer professional associations, government labour information, education or training providers, and direct accounts from people who perform the work.

Your prompt

Write five tasks that appear repeatedly. Circle the two that sound most engaging and mark one that may be difficult. Explain why.

Module 3 · Compare pathways

Separate requirements from common routes.

Identify credentials that are legally required, education or training employers commonly request, and alternative ways people enter the field. Compare the time, cost, admission requirements, location, and flexibility of at least two routes. A common route is not always the only route.

Your prompt

Create two columns. In the first, list verified requirements. In the second, list preferences or common experiences. Add the source for every item.

Module 4 · Ask a professional

Prepare questions that reveal experience.

An informational conversation is for learning, not asking for a job. Use public or appropriately supervised communication and respect a person’s decision not to participate. Ask about a normal week, invisible responsibilities, beginner mistakes, feedback, difficult tradeoffs, and a low-risk way to test part of the work.

Your prompt

Choose five questions from the professional conversation guide. For each, write what new evidence the answer could give you.

Module 5 · Test an interest

Choose a small experience before a major commitment.

Select a safe, realistic activity connected to one part of the work: a short course, personal project, school club, supervised volunteer task, observation, interview, or deeper research assignment. The activity should help you learn—not prove that your first idea was correct.

Your prompt

Complete: “Over the next two weeks, I will ____ because I need to learn whether ____. I will review what happened on ____.”

Module 6 · Reflect and decide

Turn evidence into a next step.

Review what you learned about interest, fit, requirements, tradeoffs, and uncertainty. Note which assumptions changed and which questions remain. Your next step may be to continue exploring, compare another field, speak with an educator, or pause until you have better information.

Final action plan

  1. One finding that increased my interest
  2. One tradeoff I need to understand better
  3. One assumption that changed
  4. One person or authoritative source to consult
  5. One action and a date to review it

Expected outcome

Leave with work you can continue.

After completing the toolkit, you should have a career-research outline, several questions for future conversations, and one realistic next action. These are learning outputs, not certifications or promises of placement, employment, admission, or volunteer-hour recognition.

Accessibility and safety

Use the material in the way that works for you.

The toolkit uses headings, lists, plain language, and keyboard-accessible HTML. You can use browser zoom, reading tools, or copy prompts into your preferred private format. Do not send Navio sensitive personal information in your answers.

Students should involve a parent, guardian, educator, or other trusted adult when they need help evaluating a source, contacting a professional, or choosing an appropriate activity.

Frequently asked questions

Before you begin.

Do I need to know which career I want?

No. Choose one possibility to practise the process. You can repeat the toolkit for other fields.

Is this career counselling?

No. It provides general career-awareness education and does not replace individualized advice from a qualified counsellor or educator.

Do I need to register?

No. The toolkit is available immediately and does not collect your responses.

Will I receive volunteer hours or a certificate?

No. The toolkit does not offer a credential or volunteer-hour recognition.

Can a teacher or family member use it with me?

Yes. The prompts work for independent use or a guided discussion.

Schools and community groups

Request a guided group discussion.
